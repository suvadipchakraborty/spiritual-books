About Ekayana

One shelf, three lamps.

Ekayana — a word borrowed from Sanskrit meaning one path — exists on a simple belief: that the Bhagavad Gita, the Quran, and the Bible each hold their own light, and that reading them shouldn't require three different apps, three different accounts, or a single advertisement between you and the words.

There's no algorithm here deciding what you should read next, no commentary shouting over the text, and no login wall. Just the verses, set in a typeface built for long reading, with your place quietly remembered for the next time you open the door.

This is not a work of theology. Ekayana doesn't rank these texts against each other or tell you what to believe — it only tries to get out of the way, so that whichever tradition you come from, or none at all, you can sit with these words on your own terms.

What's inside

Every verse of all three texts is bundled directly into this app — nothing is fetched from the internet while you read. That means Ekayana opens instantly and works with no connection at all, once it's been loaded the first time.

  • Bhagavad Gita — the complete 18 chapters, 701 verses, primarily in A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada's widely-read English rendering.
  • The Quran — the complete 114 surahs, 6,236 ayahs, in Dr. Mustafa Khattab's "The Clear Quran" — a translation made specifically for plain, modern readability.
  • The Bible — the complete 66 books, Old and New Testament, in the World English Bible (WEB) — a free, modern-English, public-domain revision of the American Standard Version.

A note on translation

These three translations were chosen for the same reason: they read in plain, current English rather than centuries-old phrasing, so someone with no background in any of these traditions can sit down and simply read. No translation replaces the original language a text was revealed or composed in — these renderings are offered as a doorway in, not the final word. One small note: the World English Bible renders the Hebrew name of God as "Yahweh" rather than "the LORD," which will look unfamiliar if you're used to the King James or NIV — this is a faithful, deliberate choice of the WEB translators, not an error.
